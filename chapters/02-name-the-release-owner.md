# Name the owner of the release

The table is full of kits. The inspector has marked several safe. The volunteer at the counter can see a queue and cannot release a kit. The updated procedure added a release step, but nobody on the shift knows who can make that decision while the shift lead is away.

You will separate doing work, making a decision, and being accountable for an exception. A role chart is useful only when it explains what happens on an abnormal day.

## Draw transitions, not boxes full of titles

For this exercise each kit can move in this order:

```text
prepared → passed inspection → authorized for release → handed to a borrower
                    ↓ if failed
               hold for repair
```

A kit is not in two states at once. A failed inspection cannot be “cleared” by a release signer. Inspection asks whether the kit passes a specific safety procedure. Release authorization asks whether the inspected kit can be matched to a confirmed request within the rules. Pickup records the actual transfer. These distinctions give you a way to diagnose where work accumulates.

| Transition | Who does it | Evidence required | Who handles an exception? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unbuilt to prepared | Prep crew | Checklist with kit ID | Prep coordinator |
| Prepared to passed | Trained inspector | Completed, passing safety check | Inspection lead; failed kit stays held |
| Passed to authorized | Designated release owner | Passing inspection and confirmed pickup | Shift lead or explicitly named delegate |
| Authorized to handed off | Counter volunteer | Kit ID matches the request | Counter lead contacts release owner; no bypass |

This is more informative than writing “everyone owns safety.” Assigning an operator is not enough. A decision must have an actual authority and an escalation when the person cannot respond. A backup gets only the authorization already granted by the organization's rules. If there is no authorized backup, record the queue and wait. Staffing pressure is not permission.

## Two handoffs, two problems

Imagine eight passed kits accumulate while release capacity is briefly unavailable. Adding another inspector makes the passed-but-unreleased queue larger. Now imagine no kit passed because a batch failed the inspection. Adding a release signer does not make an unsafe batch releasable. Both days show a lonely counter. They require opposite inquiries.

Role design has a cost. Two separate people can protect an important decision from haste. The same separation can reduce availability if there is only one qualified person per step. Decide deliberately where independence matters, then provide coverage and a documented handoff rather than removing the protection by accident.

## Try it

A trained inspector leaves early. Three units are already signed as passed and six prepared units are waiting. The release owner is present. Draw which units can continue and name who resolves the rest.

**Check.** Three may continue if they meet the other release requirements. The six wait for an authorized inspector, not an enthusiastic counter volunteer. Write in the next shift's record who returns, which kit IDs remain held and when the shift lead will check again.
