# Count the handoff, not just the preparation

Suppose a volunteer team loans simple repair kits at a library counter. On Saturday it prepares 24 kits and hands 20 to confirmed borrowers. The next Saturday it prepares 24 again. A new release process is in place. This time only 14 reach borrowers. Both preparation counts are true. A dashboard showing just that count would tell you nothing had changed.

This chapter shows how to choose an operational outcome, distinguish it from a proxy, and record a decision you can reopen. The case and all its figures are fictional.

## What was the work for?

Preparing a kit is an activity. A safe kit reaching a borrower is an outcome of the team's operations. The distinction changes the unit of observation: a preparation log records a checked-in inventory item; a handoff log records a confirmed borrower receiving a particular kit. Neither count establishes that the repair succeeded. If the purpose is helping more repairs happen, the handoff is still an intermediate result, but one the team can observe directly without surveying borrowers about personal circumstances.

Define the result precisely for these Saturdays: **unique confirmed requests filled with an inspected kit by the counter's closing time**. Keep confirmed requests in the denominator even if a borrower misses the window. If you also count attendance or completed arrivals, describe those separately; deleting unserved requests from the denominator changes the question.

| Window | Confirmed requests | Kits prepared | Recorded handoffs | Filled share |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Before the change | 24 | 24 | 20 | 20/24 = 83.3% |
| After the change | 24 | 24 | 14 | 14/24 = 58.3% |

The filled share decreased 25 percentage points. There were six fewer handoffs. None of those calculations makes the new procedure the established cause. First check that the windows, definitions and logs are comparable. Then ask which stage changed, what else changed, and whether the recorded process can explain who went unserved.

## Record the smallest decision

A good decision note fits on a card:

- **Question:** Do we change the release handoff for the next window?
- **Outcome:** Unique confirmed requests filled before closing.
- **Constraints:** No uninspected release; no unnecessary borrower details.
- **Evidence:** Intake, inspection, release and pickup time or counts by status, with the same window boundaries.
- **Owner and revisit trigger:** The shift lead reviews at noon if the inspected-but-unreleased queue cannot clear before close.

This note does not commit the manager to a remedy before comparing the upstream and downstream work. It commits the manager to finding out if another prep volunteer would help. That is a much narrower and more useful promise than “increase productivity.”

## Try it

With 18 handoffs and 24 confirmed requests, report the filled share. If six requests expired without a handoff, what happens to the reported share when you remove them? Explain the harm in one sentence.

**Check.** The share is 75%. Deleting the six unserved requests makes it 100% for the changed denominator, not for the original cohort. A favorable metric can conceal a real access problem even when the arithmetic is immaculate.
