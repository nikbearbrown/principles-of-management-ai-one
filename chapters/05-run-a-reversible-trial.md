# Run a reversible trial

Fourteen kits changed hands last Saturday. The team wants to announce a redesign. It has not decided what it will count or what would make it stop. A manager does not need a grand plan for a modest operational problem. A short trial needs a purpose, a protection, and a way to find out if it helped.

You will design one change, write a decision rule in advance, and separate adopting a procedure from improving service.

## Pick one change the team can undo

Test a scheduled second qualified release owner for two comparable windows. Leave the inspection rule, pickup eligibility and kit types alone. Keep an explicit log of signoffs and waits. Tell volunteers when the backup can act and who still owns exceptions. In the example, if everything else holds, the ceiling rises from 14 to 18. It may fail to do that if demand, kit supply or counter staffing changes. A projection is not an observed result.

Record three distinct measures:

- **Adoption:** For how many windows did the assigned backup actually cover the agreed slot?
- **Outcome:** How many of all confirmed requests received an inspected kit before close?
- **Safety and workload:** Any unauthorized release, hold bypass, unreasonable wait or unscheduled overtime?

Outside this fictional case the team would agree on definitions, real staffing and what data it may retain. The case uses kit and request IDs; no sensitive borrower details are needed.

## State what counts before seeing the result

For a bounded example, pause immediately on any safety bypass. Otherwise continue for a second week only if the backup role is staffed and at least 17 of 24 confirmed requests are filled without adding unscheduled hours. Keep requests in the denominator and report capacity shocks. This is a management rule for a small tryout, not a statistical test or a public performance promise. If expectations were missed because an inspection batch failed, review the failed assumption as well as the count before changing policy.

A completed training or a signoff that everyone uses the new form is adoption evidence. It does not establish that the queue moved. If the backup was staffed and handoffs rose, improvement is descriptive until the team can rule out plausible changes in equipment, demand, volunteer experience and appointment schedule. A better comparison could vary coverage over more comparable windows and record deviations. The aim here is to decide responsibly under uncertainty, not to manufacture a causal conclusion from two Saturdays.

## Do a short after-action review

1. Say what was expected before the window.
2. Compare actual start conditions, staffing, queue times and handoffs.
3. Explain whose observations would contradict the team's preferred story.
4. Describe what you will keep, change or undo and name the next check.

The [turnaround review](../practice/turnaround-review.md) asks you to carry out that argument on an abnormal week. Work without a model first. If you ask one to critique your plan, share only the fictional case and record one reasoned rejection or revision. Do not reward a polished memo that cannot explain which kit can move next.
