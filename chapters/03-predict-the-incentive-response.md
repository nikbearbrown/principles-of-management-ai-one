# Predict the response to an incentive

Suppose a team is praised for the number of kits it prepares. More prepared kits are useful when prep is the constraint. The same praise after the bottleneck moved rewards work piling up before release. Volunteers do not have to be dishonest for a metric to become a bad incentive.

The task is to compare incentives by the behavior a reasonable person might choose, the side effects that may follow, and the evidence that would change the design.

## Look for the easiest way to succeed on paper

Call one plan **prep count**. At the end of the window it reports the number of kits with a completed preparation checklist. If praised on that measure, a crew might start another batch instead of helping the authorized release owner reconcile incomplete reservations. The work is real. It just did not move the constrained step. Expect the upstream queue to increase without more handoffs if release stays full.

Call the other plan **safe fill with a guardrail**. The outcome is the share of confirmed requests filled during the window. Guardrails are passing inspection, no unauthorized release, and visibility into deferred requests. Before praising the group, inspect wait time, cancellations and held units. This design can have its own bad response: choosing only borrowers who are easy to reach or discouraging complex requests. A count of services delivered is not a measure of fairness.

| Design | Observable upside | Plausible side effect | Independent check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reward more preparations | More completed prep checklists | Inventory piles up at the next step | Record passed and authorized queues at the same closing time. |
| Reward safe fills | More safe handoffs among confirmed requests | Intake selectively drops hard cases | Compare original intake cohort to the pickup log and report all exclusions. |

A prediction is not a claim about how volunteers actually feel. State what would count against it. If handoffs rise after a preparation increase while release slack remains, prep may really have constrained this week. If staff explain that queues accumulated because a person was absent, a new score may do less than coverage. Ask volunteers which work the score overlooks, but keep their comments separate from counted outcomes.

## Motivation is not just a score

Some people value autonomy, mastery, a predictable shift or a clearer explanation of why their work matters. Treat these as hypotheses you can ask about, not boxes to assign to each person. A stewardship measure is useful while it gives the team feedback. It becomes harmful if people avoid difficult cases or hide problems to protect the number. Praise reporting an exception as useful evidence even when the target was missed.

## Try it

Write a prediction for a “fastest pickup” leaderboard and one measurement that could contradict the prediction. Then propose a change other than replacing the leaderboard with another ranking.

**Example answer.** Faster recorded pickups might hide delays before a pickup is logged. Time from request confirmation through handoff, keeping unfilled requests visible. Try a clear appointment window and explain how exceptions are escalated before changing individual incentives.
